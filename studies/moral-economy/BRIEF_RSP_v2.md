# Two-pager — what the RSP paper says and why anyone should care

Companion brief to `PAPER_RSP_v2.md`. Every number here comes from the manuscript's generated tables;
nothing was retyped from memory. Not for submission — this is a communication document.

## The one-sentence version

The Catholic Church has been arguing about wages, property and poverty since 1891, and when we read
five years of Croatian digital media to see how much of that argument actually reaches public debate
about the economy, the answer is very little, the little that gets through is mostly about climate,
and the one idea with the most to say about poverty is almost entirely missing.

## What the study is

A tradition that has written for 130 years about work, wages, property and the poor exists as a body
of documents. Whether any of it circulates in public argument about the economy is a separate
question, and nobody had measured it. The study measures it.

The material is 710 307 Croatian digital media posts from 2021 to 2026, gathered around the topic
rather than around a list of outlets, so Church media and secular news portals sit side by side in
the same pile. Inside that pile, 108 966 posts have religion and economics in the same breath. Of
those, 1 198 also name the Church's official teaching, either a document by title or one of a handful
of expressions that mean nothing outside Church teaching.

Two things make the study unusual. It reads the whole corpus rather than a sample, so the figures are
counts and not estimates. And it does not trust its own search. Two rounds of hand coding, 810 items
in all, asked whether the machine was right, and the answer changed the headline. Only 39,5% of the
apparent religion-meets-economics posts are real encounters. The rest are homonyms and coincidence, a
church named as a street, a devotional article containing an economic word, *zadužen za* meaning in
charge rather than in debt. Dividing by the honest denominator roughly triples the rate the study
reports, from about one in ninety-one to about one in twenty-seven. The paper argues against its own
first number and publishes the correction.

## What it shows

**Naming the teaching is rare.** Teaching appears in roughly one of every twenty-seven genuine
meetings between religion and economics. Across five years of an entire national media system, this
is a thin stream.

**It is lopsided by subject, and in the wrong direction.** Climate and energy carries the highest
rate at 14,80%, comfortably ahead of everything else. Housing, prices and the euro changeover are at
or near zero. Wages sit at 3,79%. The tradition began with wages and only reached the environment in
2015, so the subject the Church has written about longest is not the one that travels, and the
newest one is.

**The sorting happens outside the Church.** Split the posts by the kind of outlet they appeared in
and the pattern separates. Inside Church media, the subjects are covered at fairly even rates and
climate leads by only 1,40×. Inside secular media, climate leads by 3,79×, or 4,36× under the reading
least favourable to the argument. The Church says roughly the same amount about each of its subjects.
The secular press decides which of them to carry, and the decision is made at the border.

**What determines the decision looks like translatability.** Climate already has a secular argument
running, made of science, cost, policy and risk, so an encyclical can be attached to a story the
newsroom is already writing. The just wage has no equally available secular vehicle in Croatian
debate, so teaching about it would have to stand on Church authority alone, and much less of it gets
through. A rival explanation, that editors simply prefer the current pope, was tested and failed:
Francis-era teaching appears in secular outlets 21,2% of the time and older teaching 26,1%, which is
the same. Editors select on the subject, not on the vintage.

**On poverty, what circulates is help rather than rights.** Poverty is the biggest contact zone
between religion and economics in the corpus and secular media do carry it. But the tradition's own
principle for exactly this subject, *opcija za siromašne*, the preferential option for the poor,
appears in 33 posts out of 710 307. The universal destination of goods, the tradition's sharpest
claim about property, appears in eight. What circulates instead is the headline nomenclature, the
name of the teaching itself in 472 analysed posts and *Laudato si'* in 447.

## Why it matters

Social policy is defended in public, and it is defended in language. It matters enormously whether
poor people are described as persons who ought to be helped or as persons who are owed something.
The first makes assistance a matter of generosity, and generosity arguments lose at budget time
because they can be answered by listing what has already been given. The second makes it a claim,
which cannot be answered that way. Framing research shows the difference moves who the public blames
for poverty, and deservingness research shows that a public speaking the language of help keeps
asking which of the poor qualify.

Croatia is where this bites hardest. The Church is among the most trusted institutions and the
largest provider of welfare outside the state. Social assistance is narrow and heavily means-tested.
And Croatians favour redistribution more than most Europeans, yet the moral vocabulary available to
them for arguing about the poor is overwhelmingly the vocabulary of assistance. Support for
redistribution exists with no ready route by which to become a demand.

The finding is not a criticism of the Church, and the paper is careful about this. The selection
happens outside the Church's hands, in newsrooms doing what newsrooms normally do. It is also not
about belief. A diocese could preach the option for the poor every Sunday and none of it would show
up here unless a news portal printed the phrase.

## How to communicate it publicly

Lead with the vocabulary, not with the religion. The story is that a country arguing about poverty
has inherited a language of helping and lost a language of owing, and that this can now be counted
rather than asserted. Thirty-three posts in five years is the number that lands.

Second, lead with the climate paradox. A Church that has written about wages since 1891 and about
the environment since 2015 now reaches the public mostly on the environment. That is immediately
graspable and it opens the real point, that the press carries a moral argument only when it already
has a secular story to hang it on.

Third, keep the frame away from the two traps. This is not a story about the Church failing to
communicate, and it is not a story about religion intruding on public life. It is a story about how
any moral tradition gets admitted to secular debate, and Catholicism happens to be the case with a
long enough paper trail to measure.

Fourth, use the correction as a credibility asset rather than hiding it. "We checked our own search
by hand, found that most of what it caught was coincidence, and the honest number turned out to be
three times bigger than our first one" is a better story than any single statistic, and it inoculates
against the obvious objection that a keyword search proves nothing.

For channels, the sequence that fits is a short op-ed in a national daily built on the assistance
versus entitlement contrast, a chart-led version for the DigiKat site showing the subject gradient
and the confessional-secular split, and a policy-brief version for the social policy community
built on the Croatian means-testing implication.

## The most interesting bits

The self-referential loop is the nicest detail in the paper. The journal it is submitted to ran a
line of work on the Church and welfare between 1994 and roughly 2006 and then dropped it, and the
scholar most often named inside the analysed posts, with 116 mentions across 67 posts, is one of that
line's own contributors. The corpus is quoting the journal back at itself.

The old documents behave better than expected. Recency explains a lot but not everything, because
documents from 1891 to 1991 account for 0,8% of climate's invocations and for 11,5% to 14,3% of
those in business, wages and housing. Old teaching still gets quoted on the subjects it was written
for. It simply has no purchase on the subject it never addressed.

The failed prediction is worth telling. The expectation was that secular outlets would prefer recent
teaching, and they do not. Reporting an expectation that did not survive is what makes the surviving
ones worth believing.

Two Croatian homonyms nearly derailed the search and were caught by reading. *Supsidijarna zaštita*
is a form of asylum status and matches the pattern for subsidiarity, and *socijalne nauke* means
social sciences and shadows *socijalni nauk*. One false match in 150 posts and three in 5 072. These
are small numbers that exist only because someone looked.

And the honest weak point, which should be stated wherever the study is presented rather than waited
for: the hand coding was done by a language model working from a codebook written in advance, not by
two human coders. It shows a codebook can be applied consistently. It does not show that two people
would agree. Replacing it is the first thing on the extension list.
