# Part A — Why this paper needs a literature at all

The empirical result is descriptive and, on its own, could read as a curiosity: *a religious tradition
does not quote itself very much, and quotes itself unevenly*. The literature does three jobs:

1. It converts "uneven" into **theoretically expected vs. surprising** — the tradition is quoted *least*
   where it is *deepest*, which no existing account predicts.
2. It supplies the mechanism for the boundary finding — why doctrine crosses into secular media on one
   topic only.
3. It supplies the **social-policy payoff** that makes the paper publishable in RSP rather than in a
   religion journal: a charity vocabulary and an entitlement vocabulary have different consequences for
   what a welfare state is allowed to be.

# Part B — Five literatures

## B1. Religion in the public sphere — the terms of entry

The question is not whether religion is present in public debate (it obviously is in Croatia) but on
**what terms** it is admitted.

| Ref | Contribution | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Casanova, J. (1994). *Public Religions in the Modern World*. University of Chicago Press. ⚠️ | Deprivatization: religion re-enters public argument rather than retreating to private conscience | Frames the puzzle in §1: if religion has deprivatized, we should observe it arguing *as religion* |
| **Habermas, J. (2006). Religion in the Public Sphere. *European Journal of Philosophy*, 14(1), 1–25.** ✅ **verified 2026-08-04** (DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0378.2006.00241.x) | The **translation proviso** — religious contributions enter secular public debate only once rendered in generally accessible reasons | **The paper's central theoretical device.** Our finding is that translation succeeds only where a secular argument already exists to translate *into* |
| Herbert, D. — religion, publicity and mediated public sphere ❓ | Mediated conditions of religious publicity | Optional; drop if space is tight |

> **Title caution.** Some secondary sources render Habermas (2006) as "Religion **and** the Public Sphere".
> The Wiley record and PhilPapers both give "Religion **in** the Public Sphere". Use *in*.

**Why Habermas is the right spine.** The translation proviso predicts that doctrine will be admitted in
proportion to how easily it maps onto an available secular vocabulary. Climate has a fully-formed secular
argument (science, policy, cost); the just wage and the universal destination of goods do not have an
equally available secular counterpart in Croatian public debate. **This is the mechanism, and it is
testable in shape if not in cause.** It also lets the paper stay descriptive: we observe the outcome the
proviso predicts, without claiming to have identified the translation act.

**Deliberately NOT used:** Hjarvard's mediatization-of-religion framework. It is the natural frame for a
media-studies venue and the wrong one for RSP — it makes the paper about media logic rather than about
welfare vocabulary, and it costs 300 words RSP has no room for. It moves to the second paper.

## B2. Religion and the welfare state — why the *content* of doctrine matters for policy

This is the literature that makes the paper RSP's business.

| Ref | Contribution | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Kahl, S. (2005). The religious roots of modern poverty policy: Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed Protestant traditions compared. *European Journal of Sociology*, 46(1), 91–126.** ✅ | Catholic doctrine produced **subsidiarity-based** poverty policy: obligation falls first on family, parish and association, and only residually on the state | **The single most important reference in the paper.** It supplies the prediction our data confirms: a Catholic public vocabulary should be one of obligation-to-help, not of state entitlement |
| van Kersbergen, K. & Manow, P. (eds.) (2009). *Religion, Class Coalitions, and Welfare States*. Cambridge UP. ⚠️ — reviewed in **RSP 17**(3), 2010. ✅ | Confessional politics as a determinant of welfare-regime type | Places the paper in a literature RSP has already engaged |
| van Kersbergen, K. (1995). *Social Capitalism*. Routledge. ⚠️ — reviewed in **RSP 3**(1), 1996. ✅ | Christian democracy and the social-market settlement | Secondary |
| Manow, P. — religious cleavages and welfare-state formation ❓ | — | Optional |

**The argument this enables (§7).** If Kahl is right that Catholic doctrine historically produced a
subsidiarity-shaped poverty regime, then a Catholic public vocabulary that speaks of the poor as objects
of care rather than bearers of claims is not a failure of the tradition — it is the tradition working as
designed. The finding therefore has a policy edge: **in a country where the Church is the largest
non-state welfare actor and among the most trusted institutions, the moral vocabulary in public
circulation is structurally biased toward residual, discretionary provision.**

## B3. Framing poverty — charity vs. justice, made precise

This literature is what stops §6 from being an impressionistic reading.

| Ref | Contribution | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Iyengar, S. (1990). Framing responsibility for political issues: The case of poverty. *Political Behavior*, 12(1), 19–40.** ✅ **verified 2026-08-04** (DOI 10.1007/BF00992330). Companions: Iyengar (1991), *Is Anyone Responsible? How Television Frames Political Issues*, University of Chicago Press ⚠️; Iyengar (1996), *Annals of the AAPSS*, 546 ⚠️ | **Episodic vs. thematic framing.** Episodic framing (the individual case) produces individual attributions of responsibility; thematic framing (the structural context) produces societal ones | **The conceptual backbone of §6.** Charity language is the moral analogue of episodic framing; justice language the analogue of thematic framing |
| **van Oorschot, W. (2000). Who should get what, and why? On deservingness criteria and the conditionality of solidarity among the public. *Policy & Politics*, 28(1), 33–48.** ✅ **verified 2026-08-04** | Five deservingness criteria — control, attitude, reciprocity, identity, need — as the layer mediating between social position and policy preference | Charity framing *requires* a deservingness judgement; entitlement framing suspends it. Lets §7 name the mechanism without estimating it |
| Boukes, M. (2022). Episodic and thematic framing effects on the attribution of responsibility. *The International Journal of Press/Politics*. ⚠️ | Contemporary replication and extension | One line; shows the mechanism is live, not a 1990 curiosity |
| Feagin, J. / Kluegel & Smith — individualistic vs. structural attributions of poverty ❓ | The attitudinal literature behind Iyengar | Cite at most one |

> **CARIN attribution — get this right.** The five criteria are van Oorschot's (2000). The **acronym
> "CARIN" and the measurement scale are later**, from Meuleman, Roosma & Abts, *Social Science Research*
> (2020) ⚠️. Do not attribute the acronym to van Oorschot 2000. If the paper only needs the criteria —
> which it does — cite van Oorschot alone and never use the acronym.

**Crucial framing move.** We are **not** running a framing analysis — PI decision, R8 option (a). Iyengar
and van Oorschot are invoked to say *what kind of thing* our observation is and *why it would matter*, not
to claim we measured frames. The manuscript must be explicit: §6 reports the frequency of a doctrinal term
plus a qualitative reading of an inspectable set of posts. **Do not use the words "content analysis" or
"frame analysis" anywhere in §6.**

# Part C — The gap statement

> The closest prior work shows that when the secular press covered *Laudato si'*, it assimilated the
> encyclical to an already-running political and environmental argument and set its theological reasoning
> aside. That is a single-document, two-country, event-driven finding. **No study has asked whether this
> selection operates across a religious tradition as a whole** — whether some parts of a doctrinal corpus
> are systematically admitted to public economic argument while others are systematically not. Doing so
> requires the whole tradition as the unit of analysis and the whole media ecosystem as the observation
> window, which is what a national digital census makes possible for the first time. We supply that for
> Croatia, and find the selection runs inverse to doctrinal depth.

**One-sentence version for the abstract-adjacent paragraph in §1:** *We move from asking how one encyclical
was received to asking which parts of a 130-year tradition are admitted to public economic argument at all.*

# Part D — Propositions, and what the data says

These are **descriptive expectations**, not hypotheses in a testing sense. The design is a census with no
exogenous variation; §3 must say so and no p-values may attach to census proportions.

| # | Proposition | Derived from | Evidence | Status (post-robustness, 2026-08-04) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Explicit doctrine is rare in religion-linked economic discourse | Habermas translation proviso: untranslated religious reasons are costly to admit | 1 198 / 108 966 = **1,10%**; ceiling 10,51% counting every generic echo | Established, pending Tier-1 precision audit (R1) and linkage audit (R4) |
| **P2** | Invocation varies sharply by economic domain | — (this is the discovery) | **21×** range, 0,00%–7,15% | Established |
| **P3** | Invocation is **highest where the tradition is newest** and lowest where it is deepest | Inverts what B4 would predict | green 7,15% vs business 0,72%, wages 0,95%, housing 0,34%, prices 0,37%, euro 0,00% | **Established — green ranked first in 25 of 25 robustness variants, interval disjoint in 24 of 25** |
| **P4** | Recency alone cannot explain P3 | If it could, era shares would be uniform across domains | classical share 0,8% in climate vs 11,5–14,3% in labour/livelihood domains | Established |
| **P5** | The topical selection happens at the **confessional/secular boundary**, not inside Church discourse | Habermas; gatekeeping; the *Green Pope* precedent | secular-only 7,52% vs 1,98% next (3,8×); confessional-only 22,31% vs 15,96% (1,4×, intervals overlapping) | **Strengthened** — survives the adversarial label coding (5,16% vs 1,18%, 4,4×) |
| **P6** | In the largest contact zone — poverty — the tradition appears as **charity rather than entitlement** | Kahl (subsidiarity); Iyengar (episodic/thematic); van Oorschot (deservingness) | poverty is 37,3% of doctrinal domain-mentions, yet *opcija za siromašne* appears **29×** corpus-wide | **Suggestive by design** (PI decision R8a): term frequency + qualitative reading, explicitly labelled as such |

**Reported null (keep it in the paper):** we expected secular outlets to take up Francis-era doctrine more
readily than classical doctrine. They do not — 21,2% vs 26,1%, intervals heavily overlapping. The
selection is by **topic**, not by **vintage**. This is a genuinely informative null: it rules out "secular
media simply prefer the current pope" as an explanation for P5.
