# Which parts of a religious tradition reach public argument about the economy?
## Catholic Social Teaching in the Croatian digital public sphere, 2021–2026

---

## Abstract (English)

The Catholic Church has written about work, wages, property and poverty since 1891. Nobody has
measured how much of that reaches public argument about the economy, or which parts do. We searched
710 307 Croatian digital media posts from 2021 to 2026. Religion and economics come up together in
108 966 of them, and in 1 198 posts official Church teaching is named beside an economic term. We
read 810 of them by hand to check the machine. Only two in five of the meetings it finds are
genuine, and five in six of the teachings it detects are genuine. Corrected for both,
teaching is named in about 1 of every 27 genuine meetings. It is named most on climate and energy
(14,8%) and least on housing, prices and business. The secular press makes this pattern, not the
Church. Inside Church media it is spread evenly across subjects. It travels when a secular
argument is already there to carry it. On poverty, the largest subject, the words that circulate are
words of help, not words of right.

**Keywords:** socijalni nauk Crkve; siromaštvo; socijalna politika; mediji; javna sfera; Hrvatska

---

## 1. Which parts of a tradition reach public argument?

This journal opened its first issue by asking what Catholic Social Teaching could contribute to
Croatian social policy (Valković, 1994.). At the time that was a question about what ought to
happen, and there was no way to check what was actually happening. Now there is.

This matters more for politics than for religion. Social policy has to be defended in public, and
defending it means using words. It makes a difference whether poor people are described as people
who should be helped or as people who are owed something. In the first case, help is a choice. In
the second, it is a claim. That difference shapes how a cut to social assistance gets discussed,
either as a state being less generous or as a state taking away something it owed. It also decides
who is allowed to object.

Croatia is a good place to ask which of the two is being used. The Catholic Church is one of the
most trusted institutions in the country, and it is the largest provider of welfare outside the
state. Croatians favour redistribution, and they live with fairly high income inequality (Šućur,
2021.). Here religion is a public voice with something to say, not a private matter (Casanova,
1994). So the question is not whether the Church speaks. The question is which of its subjects the
rest of the media pass on. Whatever moral language ends up in public, its shape tells us something
about Croatian social policy, not about religion.

To find out, we searched every post in a large body of national digital media, and we report three
results. The first says where the sorting happens. Which subjects carry teaching is settled at the
boundary between Church media and secular media, not inside Church talk, and it depends on whether a
secular argument is already running that can carry it. The second says which subjects those are.
Teaching is concentrated on climate and energy, the newest subject the Church writes about, and thin
on work and wages, the subjects it started with. The third says how much of it there is, and the
answer is a small share of economic argument that stays small after correction.

The closest earlier study asks a smaller version of this question. Pou-Amérigo (2018) looked at how
US and UK newspapers covered *Laudato si'*. The papers fitted the encyclical into a political and
environmental argument that was already running, and left the theology aside. That study covers one
document, two countries and ten days. Nobody has asked whether the same sorting happens to a whole
religious tradition, letting some parts of it into public argument about the economy and keeping
others out. To answer that you need the whole tradition on one side and a whole media system on the
other. So we move on from how one encyclical was received, and ask instead which parts of a
130-year tradition reach public argument about the economy at all.

## 2. What the tradition says, and what this journal has said about it

Catholic Social Teaching is the set of official Church documents about economic life. It usually
starts with *Rerum Novarum* (1891). For its first hundred years it was about workers and owners,
about the just wage, the right to form a union, private property and the duties that come with it,
and the dignity of work (*Quadragesimo Anno*, 1931; *Laborem Exercens*, 1981; *Centesimus Annus*, 1991). The
environment arrives late. *Laudato si'* (2015) and *Laudate Deum* (2023) are the newest economic
subject in the whole tradition, and *Fratelli tutti* (2020) takes the older argument about
brotherhood into questions of property and who gets what. The *Compendium of the Social Doctrine of
the Church* (2004) lists the principles, which are human dignity, the common good, subsidiarity,
solidarity, the universal destination of goods and the preferential option for the poor. We built our search
terms from those words.

Three of those principles matter here, because they point in two different directions. The universal
destination of goods says that the goods of the earth are meant for everyone, and that private
ownership exists to serve that purpose. The preferential option for the poor says that how the poor
are doing is the test of whether an arrangement is any good. Both of these say the poor are owed
something. Subsidiarity says something else. It says a job belongs to the smallest group that can do
it, which for helping the poor means the family, the parish and the local association. That one says
the poor should be helped. Both ideas are genuinely Catholic. Which one the media pick up is a
separate question, and counting can answer it.

We use this list of documents only as background. We do not score documents for how deep or
important they are, and we do not claim that deeper documents get quoted more often. The list settles
one thing nobody argues about, which is the year the Church started writing on each subject. That is
all Section 4 needs.

Two lines of research make this a question about social policy rather than about religion. Kahl
(2005) traces modern poverty policy back to religious roots. Catholic teaching, he shows, built a
system around subsidiarity, in which helping the poor is first the job of the family, the parish and
the local association, and only then of the state. Lutheran and Reformed countries arranged it
differently. The second line of research compares religion and welfare states (van Kersbergen &
Manow, 2009) and has already been discussed in this journal. It shows that where Christian
democratic parties built the welfare state, they built one that pays money out to families but
provides few public services. Both point the same way. Both expect a Catholic public language in
which the poor are owed help, not one in which they are owed rights. Nobody has yet measured that
language where it is actually used.

Between 1994 and about 2006 this journal ran a steady line of papers on the Church, welfare and
social policy, and then it stopped. Valković (1994.) began it. Šagi (1995.) applied it to Croatian
conditions. Zrinščak (1995.) placed religion in the third sector in Central and Eastern Europe.
Valković (1996.) called solidarity and justice the foundations of the welfare state. Three papers in
1997 took up solidarity and subsidiarity (Anić, 1997.; Marasović, 1997.; Šarić, 1997.). Baloban
(2005.) traced Croatian social work back to the charitable work of the Church. This paper answers
that line of work rather than starting a new one, and it turns a question about what ought to be
into a question we can measure. One small detail shows how close the connection is. The Croatian
scholar named most often in our teaching posts is Baloban, with 116 mentions across 67 posts. The
corpus is partly quoting this journal's own authors back to us.

## 3. Data and how we measured

We use the DigiKat corpus, which holds 710 307 Croatian digital media posts from 2021 to 2026,
collected by a media monitoring service. It was built around a topic rather than around a list of
sources, so Church media and ordinary news media sit in it side by side, and how posts were chosen
for it is documented with the database. Almost all of the material comes from the web, since 97,6%
of the teaching posts described below are web pages. What we call the digital public sphere here is
therefore the Croatian news portals rather than social media in general.

The analysis works with three groups of posts, each one inside the last, and Table 1 sets them out.
The first group is the corpus itself, all 710 307 posts. The second is the posts where religion and
economics come up together. To find them we wrote eleven word patterns, one for each economic
subject we track, among them taxes, wages, housing, poverty, and climate and energy. Our code calls
these eleven subjects domains. A post enters the second group when one of the patterns matches and a
religious term sits within 220 characters of that match, which is roughly two sentences on either
side, and 108 966 posts do. Because a post can raise more than one subject, we also count each post
once for every subject it raises, and we call each of those counts a pair. The 108 966 posts give
132 519 pairs. Pairs are what we count throughout, since the question is how often teaching shows up
on each subject separately.

The third group is the posts that also name official Church teaching. To find them we use a short
list of words we call Tier 1, made up of sixteen document titles and seven Croatian terms that mean
nothing outside Church teaching, among them *socijalni nauk*, *supsidijarnost* and *opcija za
siromašne*. Table A1 lists all of them. A post enters the third group when one of those words
appears in it and sits within 220 characters of the economic term as well. We ask for that closeness
so that the teaching and the economics stand in the same passage rather than at opposite ends of a
long article. The rule is a strict one. It drops 1 677 of the 2 875 posts that would otherwise
qualify, and leaves 1 198 posts carrying 1 949 pairs. These are the teaching posts, and they are
what the rest of the paper measures.

We keep one further list, Tier 2, which holds *solidarnost*, *opće dobro*, *dostojanstvo rada* and
*socijalna pravda*. These are everyday political words that anyone may use without meaning the
Church, so we never count them as teaching. They appear once in the paper, in the memo row of
Table 1, where they show how high the count could possibly go if every such word were counted, which
is 10,51% of the second group.

Some readers will expect significance tests, and there are none here. We counted every post in the
corpus, so our figures are not estimates drawn from a sample, there is no sampling error to test,
and a p-value would mean nothing. We do report margins, but they answer a different question, which
is how often our search rules find what a person reading the post would call a match. That is the
real risk in a study like this, so that is what we measured.

A word search of this kind makes two different mistakes, and we checked both by hand against rules
written down before we looked (Table 2). It can report teaching where the teaching is not really
being used, which inflates the top of our headline fraction. It can also report a meeting between
religion and economics where the two words merely happen to share a post, which inflates the bottom.

To measure the first mistake we read 150 teaching posts, picked from across the document eras and
from outlets of different sizes, and asked of each whether the teaching is really being used, which
means quoted, applied or argued from. A title sitting in a book list, a programme or an
advertisement is not being used, and we marked it wrong. The teaching is really being used in 83,3%
of the posts [76,6; 88,4], so five out of six teaching posts are genuine and one is not. We had said
in advance that we would accept the count if this figure passed 0,80, and it does, so we report the
count as it stands. Outright misreadings are rarer still, since real Church words are present in 149
of the 150 posts. The one miss is worth naming. *Supsidijarna zaštita* is a kind of asylum status,
and our pattern for the principle *supsidijarnost* picks it up. A second test points the same way.
Our commonest Tier-1 term, *socijalni nauk*, could also catch *socijalne nauke*, which means social
sciences, and it does so three times in 5 072 matches, or about 0,1%.

The second mistake is the one that changed our headline. We took 660 pairs, 60 drawn at random from
each of the eleven subjects and none of them coded in any earlier round, and asked of each a simpler
question, whether religion and economics are really addressing each other or merely happen to sit in
the same post. Only 39,5% really are, and the figure runs from 21,7% to 63,3% depending on the
subject (χ²(10) = 51,1). Two out of five apparent meetings are therefore genuine, and the other
three are not meetings at all. Some turn on words that look economic and are not, since *zadužen za*
means "in charge of" rather than "in debt", and *primanje* can be receiving a sacrament rather than
receiving a salary. Others name a church as a venue or a street, or are religious articles that
happen to contain an economic word. If we also insist that the economic word genuinely refers to
economics, the share falls to 26,8%.

That second check changes what we are able to report. Before any correction, 1 949 of the 132 519
pairs carry teaching, which is 1,47%, or about 1 in 68, and counted in posts rather than pairs it is
1 198 of 108 966, about 1 in 91. Neither figure is the one to publish, because most of what we
divided by turned out not to be a meeting at all. We divide instead by the meetings we checked and
confirmed, which is 39,5% of the pairs rather than all of them. Teaching then appears in 3,73% of
genuine meetings, about 1 in 27, and under the stricter reading in 5,48%, about 1 in 18. Correcting
therefore roughly triples the figure. We report the corrected number and show the raw one behind it.

One kind of count we deliberately do not report is teaching credited to individual popes. Our
software cannot tell *Lav* (Leo XIII, who wrote *Rerum Novarum*) from *Lav* (Leo XIV, the current
pope), or from the Croatian word for lion. Rather than publish counts we cannot clean up, we leave
them out, and nothing here depends on them. We group documents by era instead, which our search does
get right.

## 4. How often, and on which subjects

How often teaching gets named differs a great deal from one economic subject to the next, and it
differs in a direction the tradition would not lead us to expect (Table 3, Figure 1).

Before any correction, the rate runs from 7,15% on climate and energy down to 0,34% on housing and
0,00% on the euro changeover. The top is twenty-one times the bottom, and climate sits well clear of
every other subject. That ranking is not a fragile one. It survives when we allow for a few large
outlets dominating the count, for the same article appearing twice, for foreign news, for economic
words used as metaphors, for the two collection runs and for the way outlets are labelled. Climate
comes first in all 25 of the versions we had specified in advance, and in 24 of them its margin does
not touch the runner-up's. None of those versions helps with the second mistake, though, because
every one of them links religion to economics in the same way.

So the real test is to give each subject its own denominator. For each one we divide not by all
its pairs, but by the share of them that our hand check found to be genuine meetings. We settled on
doing this before we saw the coded results. The ranking holds. Climate and energy is still first, at
14,80% [11,80; 19,86], against 7,77% for the runner-up. Allowing for the coding uncertainty in all
eleven subjects at once, the chance that climate really is first is 0,987, or 0,937 under the
stricter reading. The old ranking and the new one agree at 0,87.

What the correction does change is the size of climate's lead, and we have to say so. The lead falls
from 3,25× to 1,91×, or to 1,73× under the stricter reading. The reason is straightforward.
Correcting rewards the subjects whose links were mostly false and does nothing for those whose
links were already sound. Poverty had the soundest links of any subject, 63,3% of them, because a
bishop talking about the poor really is religion meeting economics. Tax and business posts, by
contrast, are full of words that only look economic. So poverty falls from third place to seventh,
and it falls because its denominator was honest to begin with, not because teaching got any rarer
there. This leaves us with a smaller claim, that teaching is named clearly more often on climate than
on anything else. The twenty-one-fold gap of the raw table does not survive.

Being recent is not the whole answer either. Francis was pope for most of the years we cover, and
his documents lead in every subject. If recency were the whole story, though, every subject would show
the same mix of old and new documents. They do not (Table 4, Figure 2). Documents from 1891 to 1991
account for 0,8% of climate's teaching pairs, but for between 11,5% and 14,3% of the pairs in
business, wages and housing, which is fourteen to eighteen times as much. Francis-era documents
account for 88,2% of climate's. So old documents are still quoted on the subjects they were written
about, while the subject they never wrote about is carried almost entirely by documents from the
last decade. Recency clearly matters, and it just as clearly is not the only thing that matters. We
report both and do not pick a winner.

There is one thing we expected here and did not find. We thought secular outlets would carry
Francis-era teaching more readily than old teaching, on the simple idea that new things travel
better. They do not. Francis-era teaching posts turn up in secular outlets 21,2% of the time and
older ones 26,1% of the time, and those two figures sit well inside each other's margin. What the
press picks up on is therefore the subject, not the age of the document, which rules out the easiest
rival explanation for Section 5, that secular editors simply like the current pope.

## 5. Where the selection happens

To see where the sorting takes place, we split the posts by the kind of outlet they appeared in
(Table 5, Figure 3).

Inside Church media, teaching is spread fairly evenly across subjects. Climate leads at 22,31%
against 15,96% for the next subject, a gap of 1,40×, and this is the one place in all our checks
where the two margins overlap. Inside secular media the picture is much sharper. There, teaching
comes through almost only on climate, at 7,52% against 1,98% for the next subject, a gap of 3,79×. Even
on the assumption least kind to our own argument, which counts every unlabelled outlet as secular,
it is 5,16% against 1,18%, a gap of 4,36×. The two kinds of outlet also carry different amounts.
Church outlets hold 21,7% of all the pairs where religion meets economics, but 55,2% of all the
teaching pairs. Counting only the outlets that carry a label either way, the two shares are 43,0%
and 67,0%. However we draw the line, teaching words sit mostly in the Church's own outlets.

So the sorting by subject happens at the border between Church media and secular media, not inside
the Church's own talk. The Church speaks about its economic subjects at roughly similar rates. What
changes is which of those subjects the secular press passes on.

Habermas (2006) explains why. A religious argument gets into secular debate on one condition, that
it be put in reasons anyone can accept. What we find is that this works when there is already a
secular argument to put it into. Climate has one, built out of science, cost, policy and risk, and
teaching about climate can ride along on it. An encyclical about the environment can be reported as
part of a story the newsroom is already running, with the theology left in the paragraph nobody
quotes. The just wage and the universal destination of goods have no ready partner like that in
Croatian debate. Teaching about them would have to stand on the Church's authority alone, and much
less of it comes through. Pou-Amérigo (2018) found the same for one encyclical in two countries; we
see it across a whole tradition. We see the result this argument predicts. We did not watch it
happen.

Seen this way, the finding is as much about newsrooms as about the Church. Secular editors seem to
pick up a religious argument when a story they already run can carry it. That is how public debate
works in general, and there is nothing specifically Catholic about it.

## 6. Which words get used about poverty

Poverty is where religion and economics meet most often. It carries 38 232 pairs, more than any
other subject, and 727 teaching pairs, which is 37,3% of all the teaching pairs there are. Take out
the biggest outlets and poverty is second on teaching, behind climate. It is second in both secular
groups too. So secular media do carry what the Church says about poverty. The question is which part
they carry.

The Church has a principle made for this exact subject, *opcija za siromašne*, the preferential
option for the poor. It appears in 33 posts out of the 710 307, and 29 of those are posts where
religion and economics meet. It makes up 1,46% of all the Tier-1 words we found. Eight document
titles are named more often than it, and *Populorum progressio* is named just as often (Figure 4,
with the full list in Table A1). What circulate instead are the general labels. The name of the
teaching itself, *socijalni nauk*, appears in 472 posts, and its encyclical about the environment,
*Laudato si'*, in 447, while the universal destination of goods appears in five. On the Church's
biggest subject, then, what gets used are its headline names rather than its two specific principles
about who gets what.

![Figure 4](output/figures/rsp_fig4_vocabulary.png)

So far this is a count of words, together with a reading of posts that anyone can go and check. We
did not go through every post and mark each one as charity or as justice. What we can add is a
smaller hand-coded test from an earlier stage of the study (Table 6). It covers 192 links where
religion and economics genuinely meet. On poverty, 28,2% of those links read as charity and 21,2% as
justice, while the remaining 43,5% are devotional. On climate, 91,7% read as justice and none as
charity. The numbers in each subject are small, and a model did the coding rather than people, so
this supports the argument rather than proving it. What makes it worth showing is that the two
subjects at the top and the bottom of how often teaching appears are also at the two ends of this
scale, and in the direction the word counts had already suggested.

The charity column in Table 6 is the language of help; the justice column is the language of rights.
Iyengar (1990) shows why the difference matters. When coverage shows one person's case, people blame
the person. When it shows the wider system, people blame the system. The language of help is the
moral version of the first; the language of rights is the moral version of the second. The language
of help also asks a question the language of rights does not ask, which is whether this person
deserves it. People answer that question by looking at whether the person is to blame, how they
behave, what they have given back, whether they are one of us, and how badly off they are (van
Oorschot, 2000). A public
that talks about the poor in the language of help keeps that question open, and keeps asking which
poor people qualify.

The title of Valković's 1996 paper named solidarity and justice as the two foundations of the
welfare state. Thirty years on, in the largest body of Croatian digital text where religion meets
economics, the solidarity word is everywhere and the justice word, in the Church's own wording, is
almost nowhere.

## 7. Implications for Croatian social assistance

Kahl (2005) argues that Catholic teaching built a poverty system around subsidiarity. Helping the
poor is first the job of the family, the parish and the local association, and only then of the
state. If that is right, a public language in which the poor are people to be helped rather than
people with rights is the tradition doing what it was built to do. What we add is that this shows up
in the words people actually use, across a whole country, and in a country where the Church is the
largest provider of welfare outside the state.

Three things follow. None of them says the Church is doing anything wrong, and Section 5 puts the
sorting outside the Church's hands anyway.

The first is that the language actually in use suits help that is small and given at will. Croatian
social assistance is already narrow and heavily means-tested. A moral language about the duty to
help, rather than about the right to be helped, does not create that system, but it gives people
fewer tools to argue against it. The arguments left in public are arguments about generosity, and
generosity arguments have a well-known weakness at budget time, since they can be answered by
listing what has already been given. Rights arguments cannot be answered that way.

The second is that what the public wants and what the language offers do not match. Šućur (2021.)
shows that Croatians favour redistribution, measured against other European countries, and we show
what language is available to them. On one side is a public that would back redistribution. On the
other is a public conversation carried on mostly in the language of help. Put those together and the
support has no clear way to turn into a demand. A wish with no words to carry it stays a wish, and
does not become something a minister has to answer.

The third is that the idea with the most to say about policy is one of the least used. This is a
point about which words get used, not about what anyone believes or teaches. The preferential option
for the poor is not a minor idea. It is written into the *Compendium*, and it is the one idea that
turns straight into the language of rights, yet it appears in 33 posts in five years of national
digital media. The universal destination of goods, the Church's sharpest claim about property,
appears in five. This journal's founding contributors argued that the teaching should shape Croatian
social policy, and the ideas that speak most directly to policy are the ones this route carries
least.

This also shows where the real block is, and it is not the amount of teaching. Section 5 puts the
sorting outside Church talk, so what matters is whether there is a secular argument to attach to.
Climate shows that teaching travels when such an argument is already running, and Croatian poverty
debate has arguments like that already running, over whether the minimum income is enough, how far
the guaranteed minimum benefit reaches, and poverty among people who have jobs. The option for the
poor speaks to all three. The connection is there to be made, and in this corpus it is not made.
Whether it should be made is a question of values, and this paper does not answer it.

We should be just as clear about what we have not shown. We measured what moral language is
available. We did not measure what it does to opinions, votes or policy. We have no data on
individual people and nothing that works like an experiment, so there is no causal claim here and we
make none. We also see only published media text, not what is taught in a parish, a classroom or a
diocesan programme. A diocese could teach the option for the poor every week and none of it would
show up here unless a portal printed the phrase.

A word about where we stand. This work is done at a Catholic university and studies that
university's own tradition, so every detection rule is versioned and public and every matched post
can be looked at. The check that moved our headline most was the second one in Section 3, which we
wrote before we ran it and applied whichever way it came out.

## 8. Limits

What we see is news portals rather than the whole public sphere, since 97,6% of the teaching posts
are web pages. Homilies on radio and television, parish bulletins and closed social platforms are
outside our reach.

We make no claims about change over time. The corpus was built from two collection runs covering
different years, so a rise or fall from one year to the next tells us how the data was collected
rather than how much attention a subject was getting. We compare within a single run only, as a
check on what the corpus contains, and those comparisons must never be read as a trend.

The 220-character window is a choice rather than a fact. A recall check supports it, but another
number would have been defensible. It costs us 1 677 posts, and a wider window would give a larger
population.

A model did the coding rather than people. One model coded both checks, 660 items for the second and
150 for the first, working from sheets with the answers hidden. That tells us how consistently a
codebook can be applied, but not whether two independent coders would agree. This is the part of the
paper that most needs replacing. Having people double-code a slice is the obvious next step, and it
would change the width of the margins in Table 2 before it changed anything else.

A machine proposed some of the outlet labels, because 47,1% of the teaching posts carried no label
before we labelled them automatically. That is why Section 5 gives the comparison twice, once on the
narrowest reading and once on the reading least kind to our argument, rather than as a single number.

One subject shows zero, and that is not a true zero. The euro changeover has no teaching pairs at
all, but with only 407 pairs the most it could be is 0,94%, so the right way to read it is that we
did not see it, not that it never happens.

## 9. Conclusion

Thirty-two years ago this journal asked what Catholic Social Teaching could offer Croatian social
policy. Searching a whole body of national digital media lets us ask it a different way, by
measuring which parts of the teaching actually reach public argument about the economy, and what
decides which parts those are.

The answer is that everything turns on whether the teaching can be put in secular terms. A subject
carries teaching into secular argument when there is already a secular argument to attach it to, and
the sorting happens at the border between Church media and secular media, not inside Church talk.
That is what produces the pattern we see. Teaching is named in about one in twenty-seven genuine
meetings between religion and economics. It is concentrated on climate and energy, the subject the
Church took up most recently, and thin on work and wages, the subjects it started with. Being recent
is part of the story but not all of it, and no such sorting happens on the age of the documents.

For social policy what matters here is the words, not the religion. In a country where the Church is
the largest provider of welfare outside the state, the words that reach public argument about
poverty are mostly words of help, and the Church's own idea of the poor as people with rights
appears in thirty-three posts in five years. So a welfare state built on subsidiarity is argued
about, in public, in the words subsidiarity gives it. Valković (1994.) asked what the tradition
could contribute. Part of the answer is that the ideas with the most to contribute are the ones this
route carries least, and Section 5 puts the reason for that outside the Church's own communication.
