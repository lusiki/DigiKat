# Which parts of a religious tradition reach public argument about the economy?
## Catholic Social Teaching in the Croatian digital public sphere, 2021–2026

---

## Abstract (English)

The Catholic Church has written about work, wages, property and poverty since 1891. Nobody has
measured how much of it reaches public argument about the economy, or which parts do. We searched
710 307 Croatian digital media posts from 2021 to 2026. Religion and economics both appear in
108 966 of them, and in 1 198 posts official Church teaching is named next to an economic term. We
then read 810 items by hand to check the machine. Two in five apparent meetings are real, and five in
six detected teachings are real. Corrected for both, teaching appears in about 1 of every 27 real
meetings. It appears most on climate and energy (14,8%) and least on housing, prices and business.
The secular press makes this pattern, not the Church: inside Church media, teaching is spread evenly
across subjects. It travels when a secular argument is already there to carry it. On poverty, the
largest subject, the words that circulate are words of help, not words of right.

**Keywords:** socijalni nauk Crkve; siromaštvo; socijalna politika; mediji; javna sfera; Hrvatska

---

## 1. Which parts of a tradition reach public argument?

This journal opened its first issue with a question: what can Catholic Social Teaching contribute to
Croatian social policy (Valković, 1994.)? At the time that was a question about what ought to
happen, and there was no way to check what was actually happening. Now there is.

This matters more for politics than for religion. Social policy has to be defended in public, and
defending it means using words. It makes a difference whether poor people are described as people
who should be helped or as people who are owed something. In the first case, help is a choice. In
the second, it is a claim. That difference shapes how a cut to social assistance gets discussed: as
a state being less generous, or as a state taking away something it owed. It also decides who is
allowed to object.

Croatia is a good place to ask which of the two is being used. The Catholic Church is one of the
most trusted institutions in the country, and it is the largest provider of welfare outside the
state. Croatians favour redistribution, and they live with fairly high income inequality (Šućur,
2021.). Here religion is a public voice with something to say, not a private matter (Casanova,
1994). So the question is not whether the Church speaks. The question is which of its subjects the
rest of the media pass on. Whatever moral language ends up in public, its shape tells us something
about Croatian social policy, not about religion.

To find out, we searched every post in a large body of national digital media. Three results follow.
The first is about where the sorting happens. Which subjects carry teaching is decided at the
boundary between Church media and secular media, not inside Church talk, and it depends on whether a
secular argument is already there to carry it. The second is about which subjects those are.
Teaching is concentrated on climate and energy, the newest subject the Church writes about, and thin
on work and wages, the subjects it started with. The third is about how much there is. Teaching is
named in only a small share of economic argument, and it stays small after we correct for the
mistakes our search makes. On poverty, the largest subject of all, the words that get used are words
of help rather than words of right.

The closest earlier study asks a smaller version of this question. Pou-Amérigo (2018) looked at how
US and UK newspapers covered *Laudato si'*. The papers fitted the encyclical into a political and
environmental argument that was already running, and left the theology aside. That study covers one
document, two countries and ten days. Nobody has asked whether the same sorting happens to a whole
religious tradition, letting some parts of it into public argument about the economy and keeping
others out. To answer that you need the whole tradition on one side and a whole media system on the
other. So we move on from how one encyclical was received, and ask instead which parts of a
130-year tradition reach public argument about the economy at all.

## 2. What the tradition says, and what this journal has said about it

Catholic Social Teaching is the set of official Church documents about economic life. It usually
starts with *Rerum Novarum* (1891). For its first hundred years it was about workers and owners: the
just wage, the right to form a union, private property and the duties that come with it, and the
dignity of work (*Quadragesimo Anno*, 1931; *Laborem Exercens*, 1981; *Centesimus Annus*, 1991). The
environment arrives late. *Laudato si'* (2015) and *Laudate Deum* (2023) are the newest economic
subject in the whole tradition, and *Fratelli tutti* (2020) takes the older argument about
brotherhood into questions of property and who gets what. The *Compendium of the Social Doctrine of
the Church* (2004) lists the principles: human dignity, the common good, subsidiarity, solidarity,
the universal destination of goods and the preferential option for the poor. We built our search
terms from those words.

Three of those principles matter here, because they point in two different directions. The universal
destination of goods says that the goods of the earth are meant for everyone, and that private
ownership exists to serve that purpose. The preferential option for the poor says that how the poor
are doing is the test of whether an arrangement is any good. Both of these say the poor are owed
something. Subsidiarity says something else. It says a job belongs to the smallest group that can do
it, which for helping the poor means the family, the parish and the local association. That one says
the poor should be helped. Both ideas are genuinely Catholic. Which one the media pick up is a
separate question, and counting can answer it.

We use this list of documents only as background. We do not score documents for how deep or
important they are, and we do not claim that deeper documents get quoted more often. The list settles
one thing nobody argues about: the year the Church started writing on each subject. That is all
Section 4 needs.

Two lines of research make this a question about social policy rather than about religion. Kahl
(2005) traces modern poverty policy back to religious roots. Catholic teaching, he shows, built a
system around subsidiarity: helping the poor is first the job of the family, the parish and the
local association, and only then of the state. Lutheran and Reformed countries arranged it
differently. The second line of research compares religion and welfare states (van Kersbergen &
Manow, 2009) and has already been discussed in this journal. It shows that where Christian
democratic parties built the welfare state, they built one that pays money out to families but
provides few public services. Both point the same way. Both expect a Catholic public language in
which the poor are owed help, not one in which they are owed rights. Nobody has yet measured that
language where it is actually used.

Between 1994 and about 2006 this journal ran a steady line of papers on the Church, welfare and
social policy, and then it stopped. Valković (1994.) began it. Šagi (1995.) applied it to Croatian
conditions. Zrinščak (1995.) placed religion in the third sector in Central and Eastern Europe.
Valković (1996.) called solidarity and justice the foundations of the welfare state. Three papers in
1997 took up solidarity and subsidiarity (Anić, 1997.; Marasović, 1997.; Šarić, 1997.). Baloban
(2005.) traced Croatian social work back to the charitable work of the Church. This paper answers
that line of work rather than starting a new one, and it turns a question about what ought to be
into a question we can measure. One small detail shows how close the connection is. The Croatian
scholar named most often in our teaching posts is Baloban, with 116 mentions across 67 posts. The
corpus is partly quoting this journal's own authors back to us.

## 3. Data and how we measured

**The corpus.** We use the DigiKat corpus: 710 307 Croatian digital media posts from 2021 to 2026,
collected by a media monitoring service. It was built around a topic rather than around a list of
sources, so it holds Church media and ordinary news media side by side. How posts were chosen for it
is documented with the database. Almost all of it comes from the web: 97,6% of the teaching posts
described below are web pages. So "digital public sphere" here means the Croatian news portals, not
social media in general.

**Three nested layers.** A post joins the *religion-linked economic layer* when two things happen in
it: one of eleven economic word patterns matches, and a religious term appears within 220 characters
of that match. Those eleven subjects are what we call domains. One post can match more than one
domain, so the layer has two sizes: 108 966 posts, or 132 519 post–domain pairs. A pair is one post
on one domain. We count in pairs throughout, because we want to know how often teaching shows up on
each domain separately.

A post joins the *doctrinal population* — the teaching posts — when it also names a Tier-1 word.
Tier 1 is sixteen document titles plus seven Croatian terms that mean nothing outside Church
teaching: *socijalni nauk*, *supsidijarnost*, *integralna ekologija*, *opcija za siromašne*,
*kompendij socijalnog nauka*, *socijalna doktrina* and *univerzalna namjena dobara*. That word must
also appear within 220 characters of an economic term. We add this third condition so that the
teaching and the economics sit in the same passage, not just in the same long article. It is a
strict rule. It drops 1 677 of the 2 875 posts that pass the first two conditions and leaves 1 198
posts, which carry 1 949 post–domain pairs. There is also a Tier 2 (*solidarnost*, *opće dobro*,
*dostojanstvo rada*, *socijalna pravda*). Those are everyday political words, so we never count them
as teaching. We use them once, to show the highest the count could possibly go: 10,51% of the linked
layer.

**Why there are no significance tests.** We counted every post, so these are not estimates from a
sample. There is no sampling error to test, and a p-value would mean nothing here. We do give
margins, but they answer a different question: how well do our search rules find what a person
reading the post would call a match? That is the real risk in a study like this, so that is what we
measured.

**What the checks cost us.** We ran three audits, using rules we had written down first (Table 2).

*The numerator: is the teaching real?* We read 150 teaching posts, picked from across the document
eras and from outlets of different sizes. For each one we asked whether the teaching is really being
used — quoted, applied or argued from. A title sitting in a book list, a programme or an
advertisement is not being used, and we marked it wrong. We got it right 83,3% of the time
[76,6; 88,4]. We had said in advance that we would accept the count if this passed 0,80, so we
report the count as it stands. Plain mistakes are rarer still: real Church words are there in 149 of
the 150 posts. The one miss is worth naming. *Supsidijarna zaštita* is a kind of asylum status, and
our pattern for *supsidijarnost*, the principle, picks it up. One more test says the same. Our
biggest Tier-1 term, *socijalni nauk*, could also pick up *socijalne nauke*, which means social
sciences. It does so three times out of 5 072 matches, or about 0,1%.

*The denominator: is the meeting real?* This is the check that changed our headline. We took 660
pairs — 60 at random from each of the eleven domains, none of them coded in an earlier round — and
asked something simpler about each one: are religion and economics really talking to each other
here, or do they just happen to sit in the same post? Only 39,5% are really talking, and the share
runs from 21,7% to 63,3% depending on the domain (χ²(10) = 51,1). So three out of five apparent
meetings are not meetings at all. Some are words that look economic but are not: *zadužen za* means
"in charge of", not "in debt", and *primanje* can be receiving a sacrament rather than receiving a
salary. Others are churches named as a venue or a street, or religious articles that happen to
contain an economic word. If we also insist that the economic word really refers to economics, the
share drops to 26,8%.

*What this does to the headline.* Before any correction, 1 949 of 132 519 pairs carry teaching. That
is 1,47%, or about 1 in 68. Counted in posts it is 1 198 of 108 966, about 1 in 91. But we cannot
use that figure, because most of what we divided by turned out not to be a meeting at all. If we
divide instead by the meetings we checked and confirmed, teaching appears in 3,73% of them, or about
1 in 27. Under the stricter rule it is 5,48%, about 1 in 18. So the corrected number is roughly
three times the uncorrected one. We report the corrected number, and show the raw one behind it.

**We do not credit teaching to individual popes.** Our software cannot tell *Lav* (Leo XIII, who
wrote *Rerum Novarum*) from *Lav* (Leo XIV, the current pope), or from the Croatian word for lion.
Rather than publish counts we cannot clean up, we leave them out, and nothing here depends on them.
We group documents by era instead, which our search gets right.

## 4. How often, and on which subjects

How often teaching gets named differs a great deal from one economic domain to the next. It also
differs in a direction the tradition would not lead us to expect (Table 3, Figure 1).

Before any correction, the rate runs from 7,15% on climate and energy down to 0,34% on housing and
0,00% on the euro changeover. The top is twenty-one times the bottom, and climate sits well clear of
every other domain. We already knew this ranking was solid. It holds when we correct for a few big
outlets dominating the count, for the same article appearing twice, for foreign news, for economic
words used as metaphors, for the two collection runs and for how outlets are labelled. Climate comes
first in all 25 of the versions we had written down in advance, and in 24 of them its margin does
not touch the runner-up's. But none of that helped with the denominator, because all 25 versions
linked religion to economics in the same way.

So the real test is to give each domain its own denominator: divide by the share of that domain's
links we checked and found real. We decided to do this before we looked at the coded results. The
ranking holds. Climate and energy is still first, at 14,80% [11,80; 19,86], against 7,77% for the
runner-up. Allowing for the coding uncertainty in all eleven domains at once, the chance that
climate really is first is 0,987, or 0,937 under the stricter rule. The old and the new rankings
agree at 0,87.

What the correction does change is the size of climate's lead, and we have to say so. It falls from
3,25× to 1,91×, or to 1,73× under the stricter rule. The reason is simple. Poverty had the truest
links of any domain, 63,3% of them, because a bishop talking about the poor really is religion
meeting economics. Tax and business posts, by contrast, are full of words that only look economic.
So correcting helps the domains whose links were mostly false, and poverty falls from third place to
seventh. Poverty falls because its denominator was already honest and had nothing to gain, not
because teaching got any rarer there. What we are left with is a smaller claim: teaching is named
clearly more often on climate than on anything else. The twenty-one-fold gap in the raw table does
not survive.

**Being recent is not the whole answer.** Francis was pope for most of the years we cover, and his
documents lead in every domain. But if being recent were all there is to it, every subject would
show the same mix of old and new documents. They do not (Table 4, Figure 2). Documents from 1891 to
1991 account for 0,8% of climate's teaching pairs, but for between 11,5% and 14,3% of those in
business, wages and housing — fourteen to eighteen times as much. Francis-era documents account for
88,2% of climate's. So old documents are still quoted on the subjects they were written about, while
the subject they never wrote about is carried almost entirely by documents from the last decade.
Being recent clearly matters. It just as clearly is not the only thing that matters. We report both
and do not pick a winner.

**One thing we expected and did not find.** We thought secular outlets would carry Francis-era
teaching more readily than old teaching, on the simple idea that new things travel better. They do
not. Francis-era teaching posts turn up in secular outlets 21,2% of the time and older ones 26,1% of
the time, and those two figures sit well inside each other's margin. So what the press picks on is
the subject, not the age of the document. That rules out the easiest rival explanation for
Section 5: that secular editors just like the current pope.

## 5. Where the selection happens

To see where the sorting takes place, we split the posts by the kind of outlet they appeared in
(Table 5, Figure 3).

Inside Church media, teaching is spread fairly evenly across subjects. Climate leads at 22,31%
against 15,96% for the next domain, a gap of 1,40×, and this is the one place in all our checks
where the two margins overlap. Inside secular media the picture is much sharper. There, teaching
comes through almost only on climate: 7,52% against 1,98% for the next domain, a gap of 3,79×. Even
if we make the assumption least kind to our own argument and treat every unlabelled outlet as
secular, it is 5,16% against 1,18%, a gap of 4,36×. The two kinds of outlet also carry different
amounts. Church outlets hold 21,7% of the linked layer but 55,2% of all teaching pairs. Among posts
whose outlet carries a label either way, the shares are 43,0% and 67,0%. However we draw the line,
teaching words sit mostly in the Church's own outlets.

So the sorting by subject happens at the border between Church media and secular media, not inside
the Church's own talk. The Church speaks about its economic subjects at roughly similar rates. What
changes is which of those subjects the secular press passes on.

Habermas (2006) explains why. A religious argument gets into secular debate on one condition: it has
to be put in reasons anyone can accept. What we find is that this works when there is already a
secular argument to put it into. Climate has one, built out of science, cost, policy and risk, and
teaching about climate can ride along on it. An encyclical about the environment can be reported as
part of a story the newsroom is already running, with the theology left in the paragraph nobody
quotes. The just wage and the universal destination of goods have no ready partner like that in
Croatian debate. Teaching about them would have to stand on the Church's authority alone, and much
less of it comes through. Pou-Amérigo (2018) found the same for one encyclical in two countries; we
see it across a whole tradition. We see the result this argument predicts. We did not watch it
happen.

Seen this way, the finding is as much about newsrooms as about the Church. Secular editors seem to
pick up a religious argument when a story they already run can carry it. That is how public debate
works in general, and there is nothing specifically Catholic about it.

## 6. Poverty: which words get used

Poverty is where religion and economics meet most often. It has 38 232 linked pairs, more than any
other domain, and 727 teaching pairs, which is 37,3% of all of them. Take out the biggest outlets
and poverty is second on teaching, behind climate. It is second in both secular groups too. So
secular media do carry what the Church says about poverty. The question is which part they carry.

The Church has a principle made for this exact subject: *opcija za siromašne*, the preferential
option for the poor. It appears in 33 posts out of 710 307, and 29 of those are in the
religion-linked economic layer. It makes up 1,46% of all the Tier-1 words we found. Eight document
titles are named more often than it, and *Populorum progressio* is named just as often (Figure 4;
the full list is in Table A1). What we see instead are the general labels: the name of the teaching
itself, *socijalni nauk*, in 472 posts, and its encyclical about the environment, *Laudato si'*, in
447. The universal destination of goods appears in five posts. So on the Church's biggest subject,
what gets used are its headline names, not its two specific principles about who gets what.

![Figure 4](output/figures/rsp_fig4_vocabulary.png)

So far this is a count of words, plus a reading of a set of posts anyone can go and check. We did not
go through all the posts and mark each one as charity or as justice. What we can add is a smaller
hand-coded test from an earlier stage of the study (Table 6). It covers 192 links where religion and
economics genuinely meet. On poverty, 28,2% of those links read as charity and 21,2% as justice, and
the remaining 43,5% are devotional. On climate, 91,7% read as justice and none as charity. The
numbers in each domain are small and a model did the coding, not people, so this backs up the
argument rather than proving it. What makes it worth showing is that the two domains at the top and
bottom of how often teaching appears are also at the two ends of this scale, and in the direction
the word counts already suggested.

The charity column in Table 6 is the language of help; the justice column is the language of rights.
Iyengar (1990) shows why the difference matters. When coverage shows one person's case, people blame
the person. When it shows the wider system, people blame the system. The language of help is the
moral version of the first; the language of rights is the moral version of the second. The language
of help also asks a question the language of rights does not: does this person deserve it? People
answer that question by looking at whether the person is to blame, how they behave, what they have
given back, whether they are one of us, and how badly off they are (van Oorschot, 2000). A public
that talks about the poor in the language of help keeps that question open, and keeps asking which
poor people qualify.

The title of Valković's 1996 paper named solidarity and justice as the two foundations of the
welfare state. Thirty years on, in the largest body of Croatian digital text where religion meets
economics, the solidarity word is everywhere and the justice word, in the Church's own wording, is
almost nowhere.

## 7. Implications for Croatian social assistance

Kahl (2005) argues that Catholic teaching built a poverty system around subsidiarity. Helping the
poor is first the job of the family, the parish and the local association, and only then of the
state. If that is right, a public language in which the poor are people to be helped rather than
people with rights is the tradition doing what it was built to do. What we add is that this shows up
in the words people actually use, across a whole country, and in a country where the Church is the
largest provider of welfare outside the state.

Three things follow. None of them says the Church is doing anything wrong, and Section 5 puts the
sorting outside the Church's hands anyway.

**First, the language people actually use suits help that is small and given at will.** Croatian
social assistance is already narrow and heavily means-tested. A moral language about the duty to
help, rather than about the right to be helped, does not create that system, but it gives people
fewer tools to argue against it. The arguments left in public are arguments about generosity, and
generosity arguments have a well-known weakness at budget time: they can be answered by listing what
has already been given. Rights arguments cannot be answered that way.

**Second, what the public wants and what the language offers do not match.** Šućur (2021.) shows
that Croatians favour redistribution, measured against other European countries. We show what
language is available to them. On one side is a public that would back redistribution. On the other
is a public conversation carried on mostly in the language of help. Put those together and the
support has no clear way to turn into a demand. A wish with no words to carry it stays a wish. It
does not become something a minister has to answer.

**Third, the idea with the most to say about policy is one of the least used.** This is about which
words get used, not about what anyone believes or teaches. The preferential option for the poor is
not a minor idea. It is written into the *Compendium*, and it is the one idea that turns straight
into the language of rights. It appears in 33 posts in five years of national digital media. The
universal destination of goods, the Church's sharpest claim about property, appears in five. This
journal's founding contributors argued that the teaching should shape Croatian social policy. The
ideas that speak most directly to policy are the ones this route carries least.

This also shows where the real block is, and it is not the amount of teaching. Section 5 puts the
sorting outside Church talk, so what matters is whether there is a secular argument to attach to.
Climate shows that teaching travels when such an argument is already running. Croatian poverty
debate has arguments like that: whether the minimum income is enough, how far the guaranteed minimum
benefit reaches, and poverty among people who have jobs. The option for the poor speaks to all
three. The connection is there to be made, and in this corpus it is not made. Whether it should be
made is a question of values, and this paper does not answer it.

We should be just as clear about what we have not shown. We measured what moral language is
available. We did not measure what it does to opinions, votes or policy. We have no data on
individual people and nothing that works like an experiment, so there is no causal claim here and we
make none. We also see only published media text, not what is taught in a parish, a classroom or a
diocesan programme. A diocese could teach the option for the poor every week and none of it would
show up here unless a portal printed the phrase.

A word about where we stand. This work is done at a Catholic university and studies that
university's own tradition. Every detection rule is versioned and public, and every matched post can
be looked at. The check that moved our headline most was the correction to the denominator in
Section 3. We wrote that check before we ran it and applied it whichever way it came out. We would
rather show our working than show a tidy result.

## 8. Limits

**News portals, not the whole public sphere.** 97,6% of the teaching posts are web pages. Homilies
on radio and television, parish bulletins and closed social platforms are outside what we can see.

**No claims about change over time.** The corpus was built from two collection runs that cover
different years. So a rise or fall from year to year tells us about how the data was collected, not
about how much attention a subject was getting. We compare within a single run only, as a check on
what the corpus contains, and those comparisons must never be read as a trend.

**The 220-character window is a choice, not a fact.** A recall check supports it, but another number
would have been defensible. It costs us 1 677 posts, and a wider window would give a larger
population.

**A model did the coding, not people.** Both audits — 660 items for the denominator and 150 for the
numerator — were coded by one model working from sheets with the answers hidden. That tells us how
consistently a codebook can be applied. It does not tell us whether two independent coders would
agree. This is the part of the paper that most needs replacing. Having people double-code a slice is
the obvious next step, and it would change the width of the margins in Table 2 before it changed
anything else.

**A machine proposed some of the outlet labels.** 47,1% of the population had no label before we
labelled it automatically. That is why Section 5 gives the comparison twice, once on the narrowest
reading and once on the reading least kind to our argument, instead of a single number.

**One domain shows zero, but that is not a true zero.** The euro changeover has no teaching pairs at
all. With only 407 linked pairs, the most it could be is 0,94%. So the right way to read it is "we
did not see it", not "it never happens".

## 9. Conclusion

Thirty-two years ago this journal asked what Catholic Social Teaching could offer Croatian social
policy. Searching a whole body of national digital media lets us ask it a different way: which parts
of the teaching actually reach public argument about the economy, and what decides which parts those
are.

The answer is that everything turns on whether the teaching can be put in secular terms. A subject
carries teaching into secular argument when there is already a secular argument to attach it to, and
the sorting happens at the border between Church media and secular media, not inside Church talk.
That is what produces the pattern we see. Teaching is named in about one in twenty-seven genuine
meetings between religion and economics. It is concentrated on climate and energy, the subject the
Church took up most recently, and thin on work and wages, the subjects it started with. Being recent
is part of the story but not all of it, and no such sorting happens on the age of the documents.

For social policy what matters here is the words, not the religion. In a country where the Church is
the largest provider of welfare outside the state, the words that reach public argument about
poverty are mostly words of help, and the Church's own idea of the poor as people with rights
appears in thirty-three posts in five years. So a welfare state built on subsidiarity is argued
about, in public, in the words subsidiarity gives it. Valković (1994.) asked what the tradition
could contribute. Part of the answer is that the ideas with the most to contribute are the ones this
route carries least, and Section 5 puts the reason for that outside the Church's own communication.
