## Abstract

Croatia's religious sector is a large non-market service provider whose prices no statistical office collects. This paper uses media coverage as an observation instrument to measure how that sector adjusted to the inflation shock of 2021 to 2026. From a corpus of 710 307 Croatian digital media posts, 8 041 mention the cost of living; coding 1 450 candidates against a fixed protocol retains 520 in which religion is genuinely connected to Croatian inflation. The sector did three things and left a fourth undone. Its leaders entered economic debate at the height of the shock in 2022, its own services were repriced two years later, and its charities absorbed demand throughout. Claims about who bears the burden of inflation, which its stated doctrine commits it to making, account for 2.9 per cent of the material. Delayed and lumpy repricing is what non-market adjustment looks like without competitive discipline.

**Keywords:** non-market pricing; inflation; religious sector; administered prices; Croatia; media as data

## 1. Introduction

Between 2021 and 2026 Croatia experienced the sharpest cost-of-living shock in a generation, with the harmonised index of consumer prices reaching an annual rate of 12.9 per cent in 2022-11, and adopted the euro in the middle of it. A large literature examines how firms, households and governments adjusted. Almost none of it examines the sector this paper is about.

The religious sector in Croatia is a service provider of considerable size. It performs marriages and funerals, celebrates masses for the intentions of paying families, maintains buildings, employs clergy and lay staff, runs schools and a national charitable network, and charges for a good deal of what it does. Its output is consumed by a large share of the population. Yet no statistical office collects its prices, no index tracks them, and no administrative source records them. The sector is economically substantial and statistically invisible at the same time.

That invisibility is not unusual. Economists routinely study sectors for which official price statistics do not exist, and the standard response is to find an observation instrument outside the statistical system. This paper uses media coverage as that instrument. A national media corpus records when the price of a mass changed, when a bishop spoke about the cost of living, and when a diocesan charity reported more people at its door, and it records these things with dates. What it cannot do is give a price index, and nothing here claims otherwise. What it can do is show the timing and the composition of a sector's response, which is what the questions below require.

We ask three things. When did the sector speak, and about what? When did it reprice? And did it make the distributional claim its own stated doctrine commits it to making, namely that the burden of inflation falls unequally?

The answers are that the sector spoke at the peak of the shock and fell quiet afterwards, that it repriced two years later, and that it made the distributional claim rarely. The gap between the timing of the speech and the timing of the price change is the paper's central empirical result. Adjustment separated by two years from the shock that prompted it is the behaviour of a price that no competitor disciplines and no contract indexes, and it is the sort of evidence about non-market price setting that is ordinarily very hard to obtain.

The paper makes three contributions. It provides the first quantitative account of how a religious sector adjusted to an inflation shock. It shows that the delay is measurable and long. And it documents, for anyone building indicators out of text, that counting posts where two topics co-occur overstates genuine engagement by roughly an order of magnitude, so linkage has to be measured rather than counted.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 Prices that markets do not set

A price that is not set by a market clearing process behaves differently from one that is. Administered prices, regulated tariffs and the fees charged by non-profit providers adjust in discrete steps rather than continuously, respond to costs with a lag, and are often held fixed for reasons that have nothing to do with cost at all. The empirical literature on price rigidity established long ago that prices change infrequently even in competitive retail settings, with median durations measured in months rather than weeks (Bils & Klenow, 2004; Nakamura & Steinsson, 2008), and that the frequency of adjustment falls further where competitive pressure is weak (Klenow & Malin, 2010). The gap widens when the price setter faces no competitor at all.

The religious sector is an extreme case of this. A parish that raises the fee for a funeral is not responding to a competitor and is not indexing to anything. It is making a decision with a distributional and reputational dimension, in an institution whose stated purpose is not commercial, about a service its users often cannot postpone. Theory suggests the resulting price path should be flat for long periods and then move in steps, and that the steps should come late. Testing that has been impossible for want of data.

### 2.2 The economics of religious provision

Work on the economics of religion has concentrated on participation, on competition between denominations, and on religion as a club good (Iannaccone, 1998). The sector's own cost structure and pricing behaviour have received far less attention, and the Croatian case almost none. What exists on Croatia examines the church's role in welfare provision and its relationship to the state rather than its prices (Kallunki & Zrinščak, 2021; Stubbs & Zrinščak, 2009), and the comparative literature on religion and welfare regimes treats the sector as a provider of services rather than as a price setter (van Kersbergen & Manow, 2009).

The non-profit sector literature offers the closest analogue. Non-profit providers price below cost, cross-subsidise, and adjust prices reluctantly because a price increase reads as a breach of mission rather than as a response to costs (Steinberg, 2006). The prediction that follows is that the adjustment, when it comes, will be large and discrete rather than gradual, and that it will be publicly contested.

### 2.3 What the sector says it will do

Catholic social teaching commits the institution to a particular position in economic hardship, which is to name the burden and say who is carrying it. The distinction the doctrine itself draws, between relieving hardship and naming its causes, maps onto a distinction that can be coded from text. Relief is charity. Naming causes is a structural claim. This gives a testable expectation, which is that a sector whose stated commitments include the second should produce material of that kind when prices rise sharply.

We treat this as an institutional-economics comparison between stated commitments and observed behaviour, not as a theological assessment. Nothing in the analysis evaluates whether the doctrine is right, and nothing evaluates the sector's pastoral conduct, which a corpus of news posts is not equipped to observe.

### 2.4 Media coverage as an observation instrument

Using text to measure economic phenomena is now standard. Dictionary-based indices of inflation attention are maintained by central banks (Aarab et al., 2025), a body of work relates media coverage of prices to household inflation expectations (Lamla & Maag, 2012), and attention has been shown to respond non-linearly once inflation crosses a threshold in the region of 2 to 4 per cent (Korenok et al., 2026; Pfäuti, 2024). That work generally treats coverage as a measure of public attention. We use it differently and more modestly, as a record of events in a sector that publishes no statistics.

The distinction matters for what has to be validated. An attention index has to be shown to measure attention. An observation instrument has to be shown to notice things when they happen. Section 4.1 does the second by asking whether coverage of the cost of living moves with actual prices, which is a necessary condition for the instrument to be recording the shock rather than something unrelated to it.

## 3. Data and methods

### 3.1 The corpus and what it is a sample of

The DigiKat corpus contains 710 307 posts from Croatian digital media published between 2021-01 and 2026-06, collected through a commercial media-monitoring service and retained by a filter that requires at least two distinct religious terms. It spans web portals, which dominate it, together with social platforms and forums, and it mixes confessional outlets with secular mainstream media.

The corpus is therefore a sample of religion-salient media content, not of Croatian media at large. Under the question this paper asks that is the right frame rather than a limitation, because the unit of analysis is the religious sector and the corpus is content about it. No quantity defined over Croatian media as a whole is estimated anywhere below, so no argument about the representativeness of the corpus for media at large is needed. Readers should not extend any figure here to media coverage in general.

One feature of the corpus governs every temporal statement in the paper. It was assembled by two different methods. An ongoing monitoring query, referred to below as method A, runs from 2021 and effectively stops in mid-2024. A later keyword-based backfill, method B, covers 2024 onward. The two overlap only in 2024, and at that overlap they behave very differently: 0.86 of the candidate posts collected by method A survive coding as genuine, against 0.21 of those collected by method B. Volumes differ as well, method B being roughly three times as dense per month. Counts are therefore not comparable across the 2024 change, and every temporal result below is reported within a method rather than pooled across the two.

### 3.2 Finding posts about the cost of living

Posts were tagged if their title or body matched an anchored vocabulary of cost-of-living terms covering inflation itself, things becoming more expensive, rising prices, the cost of living and purchasing power. The bare word for price was deliberately excluded because it fires on devotional text about the price of salvation. A guard removes figurative uses such as inflation of words or of values, voiding the individual match rather than discarding the post, so that an article which uses the metaphor and also discusses real prices is kept. This yields 8 041 posts, 1.13 per cent of the corpus.

### 3.3 Connection, not co-occurrence

A long news article can mention inflation in one paragraph and a saint's feast in another with no relationship between them. Counting such articles as evidence that the religious sector engaged with inflation would be a serious error, and Section 4.5 shows how large an error it would be.

We therefore required that a religious term appear within 220 characters of a cost-of-living mention, which produces 1 450 candidate posts. This step is deliberately generous. It is a filter for selecting material to read, not a labelling device, and it accepts false positives on the understanding that the coding stage removes them. Known collisions were suppressed first, the Croatian for Red Cross containing the word for cross, a cervical smear test containing the word for pope, a cheese named after the Trappist order, and the word for the economy being one letter from a title of the Virgin.

### 3.4 Coding

All 1 450 candidates were coded against a fixed written protocol, reproduced in Appendix B, by three independent large language model annotators with majority adjudication. Each post received four decisions. Whether it genuinely concerns the cost of living. Whether religion is genuinely connected to that content rather than incidentally nearby. Whether the inflation discussed is Croatian or another country's. And, when connected, what the post is about, which is the categorisation Table 4 reports.

Of the 1 450, coding confirms 652 as genuine connections, of which 132 concern foreign inflation, leaving 520 posts about Croatian inflation with a genuine religious connection. This is the measured set analysed throughout.

### 3.5 Validating the coding, and its limits

No human read a validation set for this study, and the paper does not imply a gold standard it does not have. Validation is model-based and is reported as such.

A stratified sample of 173 posts was recoded from the same written protocol by a different language model, with the original labels withheld and no access to them. The sample was drawn to stress the judgements the analysis depends on most heavily, so it over-represents the linkage decision, the boundary between domestic and foreign inflation, and the rarer categories. It also carries all 13 posts the original coding left unresolved. Results are in Table 7 and Section 4.6.

Two limitations follow and neither is softened here. Agreement between two language models is reliability between models, not agreement with a human reading, and the recoding model belongs to the same model family as the original annotators, so it is not the fully independent check a different family would provide. What the post is about remains the least reliable judgement, as it was in the original validation.

### 3.6 Price data

Croatian harmonised index of consumer prices annual rates of change were obtained from Eurostat for all items, for food and non-alcoholic beverages, and for energy, monthly from 2021-01 to 2026-06. Eurostat closed its previous table at 2025-12 when it migrated to the second version of the European classification of individual consumption, so the series is taken from the current table, which reproduces every one of the 60 months the earlier table also covers.

### 3.7 Artificial intelligence in the research process

Large language models performed the content coding described in Section 3.4 and the independent recoding described in Section 3.5, and assisted in drafting and in writing the analysis code. All statistical results were produced by scripts that are archived with the paper and can be rerun. The coding protocol was fixed before coding and is published in full. The author is responsible for the design, the interpretation and the text.

Before using media coverage to observe the sector, it is worth checking that the coverage moves with prices at all. Table 5 reports the correlation between the monthly share of corpus posts mentioning the cost of living and the harmonised price index, computed separately for each collection method.

Within method A, covering 2021-01 to 2024-06 with a four-month gap in 2024, the relationship is strong. Coverage correlates at 0.75 with all-items inflation and at 0.73 with food, while energy is weaker at 0.43 and diverges in 2023 when energy inflation turned sharply negative while the general price level was still elevated. The non-linearity documented in the cross-national literature appears as well. Mean coverage is 0.51 per cent of posts in months when inflation was below 4 per cent and 1.15 per cent at or above it, a jump of 2.25 times.

Within method B, covering 2024-07 to 2026-06, it is not. All-items correlation falls to 0.32 and is not statistically distinguishable from zero, food is negative at -0.52, and the threshold contrast disappears entirely, mean coverage standing at 1.26 per cent below the threshold against 1.24 per cent at or above it, a ratio of 0.98.

The natural reading is that the instrument fails the second test, and it is the wrong reading. The two windows contain very different amounts of the thing being tracked. Inflation ranges from 0.0 to 12.9 per cent within method A, with a standard deviation of 4.03. Within method B it ranges from 3.0 to 5.4 per cent, with a standard deviation of 0.60, and sits above the 4 per cent threshold in 17 of 24 months. The second window is the plateau after the shock, and a window with almost no variation in the variable of interest cannot validate anything. We report both because reporting only the first would be selective, and we draw no conclusion from the second beyond the observation that it is uninformative.

Table 6 puts the first result under three further specifications. With standard errors robust to persistence and trend, the levels relationship stands at t = 8.94. That denominator moves a good deal, monthly corpus volume within method A ranging from 4 970 to 12 860 posts, so modelling monthly post counts directly with the volume collected each month entered as an offset is a necessary check rather than a formality. It gives t = 7.06. Taken in month-on-month changes, and only between genuinely consecutive months so the 2024 gap is not differenced across, the relationship is not detectable at t = 0.43.

That last result is worth stating plainly rather than burying. Coverage tracks the level of inflation, not its monthly changes. With 35 usable differences the test is underpowered, but the more substantive point is that the threshold pattern in Table 5 is itself a statement about levels: coverage responds to inflation being high, not to it having risen slightly since last month. The instrument is adequate for dating a shock and describing a period, which is what it is used for below, and it is not adequate for month-to-month inference, which is not attempted.

The gap between the first and the third of these is the result. The sector spoke about inflation when inflation was happening, and changed its own prices two years later.

Two features of the data could produce that gap artificially and neither does. The first is the change of collection method in 2024, which is dealt with in Section 4.3. The second is that 2026 covers only January to June, so its 40 repricing posts represent half a year and cannot be compared directly with a full year. That truncation makes the late concentration of repricing stronger rather than weaker.

### 4.3 The timing survives the change in collection method

The repricing peak sits directly on the seam between the two collection methods, so the finding needs to be established without crossing it.

Table 3 does that. Restricting attention entirely to 2021 to 2023, which method A collected on its own, repricing rises from 30.3 per cent of the combined public-voice and repricing material in 2022 to 47.9 per cent in 2023. Both columns come from the same instrument in the same years, so the shift between them cannot be an artefact of assembly. The composition is already turning from speech towards prices before the seam is reached.

The overlap year read both ways confirms the direction rather than undermining it. Of the 88 posts dated 2024, 64 were collected by method A and 24 by method B. Within method A's 2024 material, repricing accounts for 93.8 per cent of the four responses. Within method B's, it accounts for 4.5 per cent, while charitable response accounts for 54.5 per cent. The two instruments were plainly picking up different things in the same year, which is exactly why the paper does not pool them, and the instrument that had been running continuously since 2021 is the one showing repricing dominant by 2024.

Beyond the seam, in method B's own material, repricing rises from 22.8 per cent of the four responses in 2025 to 83.3 per cent in the first half of 2026. The direction established inside method A therefore continues inside method B, measured independently. This is a within-instrument statement made twice, not a comparison across the seam.

### 4.4 The response the sector did not make

Claims about who bears the burden of inflation account for 15 of the 520 posts, or 2.9 per cent. They do not rise with hardship. The annual counts are 2, 1, 5, 4, 2 and 1, which is flat within sampling noise across a period in which inflation went from zero to nearly 13 per cent and back to 4.

Broadening the definition to include material in other categories that carries structural language, chiefly charity reporting that also discusses the causes of poverty, raises the figure to at most 8 per cent. The claim is marginal under either definition.

This is a comparison between what the institution states it is committed to and what is observable in the national digital record, and it should be read as bounded by what that record contains. Homilies, pastoral letters and the daily work of parish charities are structurally under-represented in a corpus of news posts, so the low visibility of distributional claims is weak evidence about what the institution said in its own settings. What it does support is that in the space where the national economic conversation happened, this particular contribution was largely absent.

### 4.5 Counting co-occurrence would have given the wrong answer

Table 1 traces the path from the corpus to the measured set. Of 710 307 posts, 8 041 mention the cost of living. Of those, the proximity filter selects 1 450 as candidates. Coding confirms 652, of which 132 concern another country, leaving 520 about Croatian inflation.

Only 45 per cent of the posts the filter selects turn out to involve religion and prices in any connected way, and the measured set is 6.5 per cent of the posts that mention the cost of living at all. Had the analysis counted co-occurrence, it would have reported the sector's engagement with inflation as roughly an order of magnitude larger than it is.

This is not a peculiarity of this corpus. Indicators built by counting keyword co-occurrence in text are common in applied economics, including in inflation measurement, and the arithmetic here suggests the practice carries a substantial and one-directional bias. The filter used above is a reasonable one and it still needs a coding stage behind it.

Table 7 reports agreement between the original coding and the blind recoding. Whether a post genuinely concerns the cost of living is reproduced on 0.931 of items, whether religion is genuinely connected on 0.892, and whether the inflation is Croatian or foreign on 0.941. What the post is about is reproduced on 0.656.

Chance-corrected agreement tells a more useful story where the categories are lopsided. The cost-of-living judgement has a kappa of only 0.299 despite high raw agreement, because more than nine in ten candidates genuinely concern prices and agreeing is easy. The connection judgement, which is the one the paper's headline count depends on, reaches 0.694, and the domestic-or-foreign judgement, which defines the measured set, reaches 0.783. What the post is about reaches 0.577, materially better than the 0.46 the original validation recorded, though still the weakest of the four.

The disagreements are informative about where the categories are soft. All 25 posts the original coding placed in the price-of-services category that appear in the sample are reproduced there, which is reassuring given that this category carries Section 4.2. The distributional category is the least stable, with 7 of the sampled posts moved to the sector-as-economic-actor category by the recoding, and 7 items from other categories moved into it. The net movement is downward, so the finding of Section 4.4 is not an artefact of over-strict coding, and if anything the strict figure is generous.

All 13 posts the original coding could not resolve were assigned by the recoding, 4 to the sector as an economic actor, 4 to no genuine connection at all, 2 to the price of services, and one each to charity, devotional content and distributional claims.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 A non-market price adjusting the way theory says it should

The interval between the shock and the repricing is the paper's most transferable finding. Public commentary from the sector concentrated in 2022, when all-items inflation averaged above 10 per cent. Coverage of the sector changing its own prices concentrated in 2024, by which point inflation had fallen to between 3 and 5 per cent, and continued into 2026.

A competitive firm facing a cost shock of that size adjusts within months. A price with no competitive discipline, no indexation clause and a reputational cost to raising it does what is observed here, which is nothing for two years and then a discrete step. The cumulative price level had risen by roughly a quarter by the time the sector moved, which is consistent with an adjustment triggered by an accumulated gap rather than by the current inflation rate.

The finding also gives an empirical handle on something usually inferred. Sectors of this kind are typically absent from price statistics precisely because their prices move so rarely that the statistical apparatus for tracking them is not worth building. The consequence is that when they do move, the move is invisible to the index and visible only in coverage of the sort used here.

### 5.2 Stated commitments and observed behaviour

The sector's stated doctrine commits it to naming the distribution of economic burdens. In the national digital record over five and a half years of the largest price shock in a generation, that claim accounts for one post in thirty-five of the material where the sector meets inflation at all, and it does not rise when hardship does.

What is present instead is the sector as an economic object. Coverage of the price of its own services and of the institution acting or commenting in economic news accounts for 373 of the 520 posts, or 72 per cent. Charity accounts for a further 17 per cent, so the sector appears far more often as a provider adjusting to conditions, and as a relief agency responding to them, than as a voice about how they are distributed.

For institutional economics this is an unremarkable finding stated precisely. Organisations act on the margins where they face costs and constraints, and their stated normative commitments are a weaker predictor of observable behaviour than their operational position is. It is worth noting that the sector's charitable response, which is costly and operational, is present throughout the period, while the normative response, which is cheap and declaratory, is not. That ordering is the opposite of what a cynical reading would predict.

### 5.3 What media coverage can and cannot observe

Coverage records what was publicly reported, which is neither the sector's conduct nor its full price schedule. Three qualifications follow.

Coverage of a price change is not a price. We can date the sector's repricing and say it was discrete and late; we cannot say by how much any particular fee rose, and constructing a price index from this material would not be defensible.

Secular outlets carry 442 of the 520 posts, or 85 per cent, and confessional outlets 14 per cent, which under this framing is a statement about the instrument rather than about the sector. The economics of the religious sector is reported from outside it, so the observation is external and, for the price-of-services category in particular, adversarial in tone. That makes it a reasonable instrument for detecting a price change, since an outlet with no stake in the sector has no reason to suppress it, and a poor one for detecting anything the sector does quietly.

The coding is model-based throughout, as Section 3.5 states. The judgements the analysis leans on hardest are the ones that reproduce best, but no human validated any of it.

## 6. Conclusion

Croatia's religious sector met the inflation shock of 2021 to 2026 by speaking during it, repricing two years after it, and running its charities throughout. It largely did not make the distributional argument its own doctrine commits it to.

The repricing delay is the finding with the widest reach. It is a direct observation of a non-market price adjusting late and in one step, in a sector where the alternative sources that would show this do not exist. That such an observation can be made at all rests on treating media coverage as an instrument for watching a sector rather than as a measure of public attention, and on measuring connection between topics rather than counting their co-occurrence, which here would have overstated the phenomenon roughly tenfold.

Two extensions follow directly. Recovering actual fee schedules from diocesan sources would convert the timing result into a magnitude. And the same instrument, applied to other sectors that publish no prices, would establish whether a two-year delay is characteristic of non-market provision or particular to this case.

## Appendix B. Coding protocol

The protocol is reproduced in full so that the coding can be replicated without access to the corpus.

An annotator sees a date, an outlet type, a headline and about 800 characters of text centred on the cost-of-living phrase that caused the post to be selected. No existing label and no other annotator's decision is shown. Four decisions follow, each conditional on the last.

Whether the post genuinely concerns the cost of living. Yes if it discusses actual prices, inflation, purchasing power or something becoming more expensive in a literal economic sense. No if the phrase is figurative, as in inflation of words or of values, or refers to something that is not a consumer price.

Whether religion is genuinely connected to that content. Yes when the religious element and the price element belong to the same statement, such as a church raising its fees or a bishop commenting on prices. No when they merely appear near one another, which happens when a religious building is used as a geographic landmark, when unrelated items sit side by side in a news roundup, when a secular organisation carries a religious word in its name, or when the text explicitly contrasts the two.

Whether the inflation is Croatian or another country's. The test is where the prices are, not where the speaker is.

What the post is about, choosing the dominant one of six categories. The price of religious services covers fees for masses, weddings, funerals, blessings and pilgrimages. The sector as an economic actor covers clergy, dioceses or the Vatican appearing in economic coverage as actors or commentators, including church finances and clergy pay. Charitable relief covers food banks, aid and collections. Who bears the burden covers structural or moral claims about the distribution of economic hardship. Devotional covers prayer or homiletic material touching hardship without an institutional or structural claim. Other covers the remainder. The distinction that most often goes wrong is that charity describes a response to hardship while a distributional claim makes an assertion about its cause.
